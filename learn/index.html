Zoning, in plain English

Zoning is the city's rulebook for every lot: what can go there, how tall, and how many homes.

Chicago sorts every lot into a district. RS districts are mostly single-family houses, RT two- and three-flats and townhouses, RM apartment buildings, B and C main streets, and D downtown. Each district caps homes with a minimum lot area per home. In RT-4 that's one home per 1,000 square feet, so a typical 25-by-125-foot Chicago lot (3,125 square feet) can hold three.

41% of the city is zoned for single-family homes or two-flats only. Allowing more on a lot takes a zoning change voted on by the City Council.

Try it in Fix Chicago HousingIts levels match real districts: 3-flat is RT-4, 6-flat RM-5, mid-rise RM-6, high-rise RM-6.5.

Sources: Chicago Zoning Ordinance, §17-2-0303 · Metropolitan Planning Council, 2023

Why the classic two-flat is often illegal to build

Many of the buildings that make Chicago's neighborhoods hold more homes than today's zoning allows on their lots.

Over the decades Chicago rezoned many neighborhoods to allow fewer homes per lot, which planners call downzoning. From 1990 to 2010, downzoned parts of the North Side and downtown added housing 1.8%, compared with 9.6% elsewhere.

Checking real lot sizes and apartment counts from the Cook County Assessor, about 83,000 of the roughly 500,000 buildings on the map hold more homes than their district allows on their lot. That counts density only. Height, setback and parking rules would flag more.

How big is the housing shortage?

The Chicago region built far fewer homes than it needed, and the gap keeps growing every year.

Up for Growth estimates the Chicago metro was 165,687 homes short in 2022. No study measures the city alone, so Fix Chicago Housing gives Chicago a share in proportion to its homes: 32.7% of the region's, or 54,141 homes missing today.

The regional gap grew by about 8,760 homes from 2023 to 2024 in Zillow's count. Chicago permitted 3,916 new homes in 2025, and the Permit Tracker counts the same total from the city's own permits.

Sources: Up for Growth, 2024 · Zillow via Mann Report · Census Building Permits Survey · Census ACS 2024 via Census Reporter

Do new apartments lower rents?

On average, yes: more homes means less competition for the ones that already exist.

A study of 11 US cities found that rents near new buildings in lower-income areas ended up 5–7% lower than rents a bit farther away. The new tenants would otherwise have competed for older apartments nearby.

Citywide, estimates of the effect differ about tenfold: from 0.19% lower rent for each 1% more new homes to about 2% lower for each 1% more homes in total. Fix Chicago Housing shows both ends of that range.

Sources: Asquith, Mast & Reed (Upjohn) · Mense, 2025 · Lauster & von Bergmann, 2024

Aldermanic prerogative

In practice, the alderperson for a ward decides whether a zoning change there passes.

It's a custom, not a law: the City Council nearly always follows the local alderperson on zoning in their ward, which lets one person approve or quietly stop a building.

A 2023 federal HUD investigation found the practice blocks affordable housing. HUD withdrew the finding in 2025.

The affordable requirement

Bigger buildings that need help from the city must set some apartments aside at lower rents.

Projects of 10 or more homes that need a zoning change, city land or city money must rent 10–20% of units below market, priced for households at 60% of area median income on average. The rest of the building pays for those units.

That's the trade-off: a higher requirement means more affordable homes in each building, but some buildings stop making financial sense. Studies of real cities mostly find little or no drop in total building; a Los Angeles model by the Terner Center finds a bigger one.

Megaprojects, planned developments and TIF

Chicago's biggest sites get their own zoning, and often lean on tax increment financing for roads and utilities.

A large site gets a planned development: zoning written for that site alone, with its own limits on height and homes. The 1901 Project's zoning, for example, allows up to 9,463 homes around the United Center, though its owners have said about 5,000.

Tax increment financing (TIF) freezes an area's property tax revenue at a base amount. Growth above the base pays for projects in the district for 23 years, then goes back to the schools, the city and the other taxing bodies. Foundry Park's TIF cleared the City Council's Finance Committee in September 2026.

Subway, L, streetcar or bus rapid transit?

They trade cost against speed: the faster and more separated from traffic, the more it costs to build.

A subway runs under the street and is the fastest and most expensive to build. An elevated L costs less but needs room overhead. A streetcar runs on rails in the street, often in traffic. Bus rapid transit (BRT) gives buses their own lanes, raised boarding platforms and fewer stops.

In Expand Chicago Transit, building costs run from about $10 million a mile for BRT to about $2 billion a mile for a subway, in today's dollars. Each figure's source is listed under Sources in the tool.

Who pays for a new line?

Most big projects combine a federal grant with state and local money.

The federal Capital Investment Grants program pays part of new lines and extensions. For large projects (New Starts) the grant usually covers up to 60%. Smaller projects under $400 million (Small Starts) can get up to 80%, with grants capped at $150 million.

The Red Line Extension has a signed federal grant agreement. The rest of a project's cost comes from state and local money, which in the Chicago region is now planned through NITA.

Sources: FTA, Capital Investment Grants fact sheet · FTA, Red Line Extension grant profile

NITA, the new regional transit agency

Since September 2026 the Northern Illinois Transit Authority sets fares, splits the money and plans service for the CTA, Metra and Pace.

The 2025 state law behind it adds about $1.5 billion a year in new money for transit, mostly from the state sales tax on fuel and Road Fund interest. Without new money, the agencies had warned in March 2025 of about 40% less service.

Fares must cover 25% of running costs across the system, and 20% from 2029. NITA's proposed 2027 budget counts about $1.3 billion in new operating money and a $9.73 billion capital program for 2027–2031.

Sources: ENR, 2025 · WTTW, March 2025 · MPC guide to the law · Metro Magazine, Nov. 2026

How the tool guesses riders

It learns from today's L stations, then asks how a new station compares.

Expand Chicago Transit fits a model to how many people entered each of Chicago's L stations on an average 2025 weekday, based on who lives nearby, how many jobs they can reach within 45 minutes, and how many buses stop there. A new station gets the people closer to it than to any other station, so it can take riders from its neighbors.

Most of the people who'd board a new line already have a station. So the tool counts as new riders only people with no L station within a 10-minute walk (half of them if a Metra station is that close), plus the share the model ties to faster trips. A line that runs next to the Blue Line, or goes where today's trains already go, adds few new riders: they'd just switch.

Models like this miss. For a typical station this one is off by about a third, so the tool shows a range, and real projects get studied for years before anyone builds them.

Try it in Expand Chicago TransitDraw a line and open “How these numbers work.”

Sources: CTA ridership data · Expand Chicago Transit: Sources
