How many homes fit on a Chicago lot?

A typical Chicago lot is 25 by 125 feet. Pick its zoning:

Chicago sorts every lot into a district, and each district caps homes with a minimum lot area per home. RS districts are mostly houses, RT two- and three-flats, RM apartment buildings. 41% of the city is zoned for single-family homes or two-flats only, and allowing more takes a City Council vote.

Legal or illegal to build today?

Each of these is a common Chicago building. Could you build it again on its lot under today's zoning?

Over the decades Chicago rezoned many neighborhoods to allow fewer homes. Checking real lot sizes and apartment counts from the Cook County Assessor, about 83,000 of the roughly 500,000 buildings on the map hold more homes than their lot is allowed today. From 1990 to 2010, downzoned areas added housing 1.8% versus 9.6% elsewhere.

How fast does Chicago need to build?

Chicago is about 54,141 homes short today, and the gap grows unless the city builds more than about 6,778 homes a year. Set the pace:

Up for Growth estimates the Chicago metro was 165,687 homes short. Chicago's share, by its share of the region's homes, is 32.7%: 54,141 homes. Chicago permitted 3,916 new homes in 2025.

Does building more lower rent?

Today's median rent is about $1,440. Add new homes to Chicago's 1.28 million:

New buildings lower rents nearby: in 11 US cities, big new buildings in low-income areas cut nearby rents 5–7%, because the people moving in would otherwise compete for older apartments. How much a whole city's rent falls is less certain, so this shows a cautious and a strong estimate.

Who really decides what gets built on your block?

A developer wants to build 40 apartments next to an L station in your ward. How many of the 50 alderpeople usually decide?

It's a custom, not a law: City Council nearly always follows the local alderperson on zoning in their ward, which lets one person approve or quietly stop a building. A 2023 federal HUD investigation found it blocks affordable housing; HUD withdrew the finding in 2025.

How much of a new building should be affordable?

Chicago asks bigger buildings to rent some homes below market. Pick the share:

Projects of 10 or more homes that need a zoning change, city land or city money must rent 10–20% of units below market. A higher share means more affordable homes in each building, but some buildings stop making financial sense. Studies of real cities mostly find little or no drop in building; a Los Angeles model finds a bigger one. The numbers above are the housing tool's middle estimate.

How do megaprojects get paid for?

With tax increment financing, an area's property taxes are frozen at today's level for 23 years, and the growth pays for roads and utilities there. Say a site pays $10 million a year today. How fast will its taxes grow?

An example, not a real district's numbers.

A large site gets a planned development: zoning written for that site alone. The 1901 Project's zoning, for example, allows up to 9,463 homes around the United Center. After 23 years the growth goes back to the schools, the city and the other taxing bodies.

Who pays for a new line?

Set what a new line costs to build:

The federal Capital Investment Grants program pays part of new lines. Big projects (New Starts) usually get up to 60%; projects under $400 million (Small Starts) up to 80%, capped at $150 million. The rest is state and local money, now planned through NITA. The Red Line Extension has a signed federal grant.

What did NITA prevent?

In March 2025 the agencies warned of about 40% less service without new money. Drag service down and see what riders lose:

Since September 2026 the Northern Illinois Transit Authority sets fares, splits the money and plans service for CTA, Metra and Pace. The 2025 law behind it adds about $1.5 billion a year. Fares must cover 25% of running costs, 20% from 2029.

Which new line gets more new riders?

Guess. Both are real tests from Expand Chicago Transit:

The tool asks one question: whose trip does the line make faster? It looks at where people near the line commute and where the people who work there live (Census commute data), times each trip by transit today, with the line, and by car, and uses how people actually get to work in each neighborhood to estimate how many switch.
